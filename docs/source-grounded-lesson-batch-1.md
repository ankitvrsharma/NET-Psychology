## 1. Sampling techniques — Unit 2

Sampling selects participants/observations from a defined population. The PowerWithin Paper 2 book's research-methodology section covers sampling; REVISATHON Part 7 has “Topic 61: Sampling”.

- **Probability sampling:** random selection with defined selection probabilities; examples include simple random, systematic, stratified and cluster sampling.
- **Non-probability sampling:** selection does not use a probability mechanism; examples include convenience, purposive, quota and snowball sampling.
- **Stratified vs cluster:** stratified sampling selects within defined subgroups; cluster sampling selects groups/clusters as sampling units.
- **Sampling vs assignment:** sampling selects participants from a population; random assignment allocates recruited participants to conditions.
- A large sample does not automatically eliminate selection bias.

**Apply:** selecting within each age group suggests stratified sampling; selecting intact schools as sampling units suggests cluster sampling. Read the procedure, not just a keyword.

**Retrieve:** How do stratified and cluster sampling differ? Why is random sampling not random assignment?

**Source trace:** PowerWithin Psychology Paper 2, research-methodology/sampling section; REVISATHON Part 7, Topic 61. Exact page and full PYQ options still require verification.

## 2. Standard scores — Unit 3, linked to Unit 2

The uploaded REVISATHON Part 3, “Topic 22: Standardized Scores”, asks learners to match scales to conventional mean and SD:

| Scale | Mean | SD |
|---|---:|---:|
| Z score | 0 | 1 |
| T score | 50 | 10 |
| Sten | 5.5 | 2 |
| Stanine | approximately 5 | approximately 2 |

Preserve “approximately” for Stanine because that is how the source item presents it. A z score expresses distance from the mean in SD units: zero is at the mean, positive above, negative below. Percentile rank expresses relative standing in a reference group; it is not interchangeable with a z score.

**Retrieve:** Which scale uses M=0/SD=1? Which uses M=50/SD=10? Why is percentile rank a different representation?

**Source trace:** REVISATHON Part 3, Topic 22; Part 7, Topic 71. The source item supports the matching targets. Check the textbook section before adding formula derivations or conversion drills.

## 3. Signal Detection Theory — Unit 5

REVISATHON Part 5, “Topic 43: Signal Detection”, explicitly contrasts observer sensitivity with response/decision bias and includes sensory threshold as a distractor.

| Actual state | Respond “present” | Respond “absent” |
|---|---|---|
| Signal present | Hit | Miss |
| Signal absent | False alarm | Correct rejection |

**Sensitivity** is discrimination of signal from noise. **Decision criterion/response bias** is how much evidence the observer requires before responding “present”. A more liberal criterion tends to increase both hits and false alarms; a conservative criterion tends to reduce both. Thus a high hit rate alone does not prove higher sensitivity.

**Retrieve:** Classify the four outcomes; explain why sensitivity and criterion are different.

**Source trace:** REVISATHON Part 5, Topic 43 and Unit 5 syllabus index. Verify the complete original item/options and textbook reference before attaching an official answer key.

## 4. Schachter–Singer theory — Unit 7

REVISATHON Part 2, “Topic 13: Theories of Emotion”, includes a component-ordering item for Schachter and Singer (1962).

**Sequence in the source item:** stimulus → general physiological arousal → cognitive appraisal/label → subjective emotional experience.

The theory combines physiological arousal and cognitive interpretation. Arousal alone does not uniquely specify the experienced emotion; the interpretation of the situation contributes to the label.

**Retrieve:** State the two factors and arrange the four components. For theory comparisons, identify the role and order of bodily response, cognition and conscious emotion instead of answering from the word “physiological” alone.

**Source trace:** REVISATHON Part 2, Topic 13; Unit 7, Theories of Emotion. Check the uploaded textbook chapters before publishing a full comparative table of emotion theories.

## 5. Social facilitation — Unit 8

REVISATHON Part 6, “Topic 53: Social Facilitation”, asks who reported the classic observation that cyclists rode faster when racing against other cyclists than when racing alone; the item names Norman Triplett.

Social facilitation concerns the effect of other people's presence on performance. The effect is not guaranteed to be positive on every task. Distinguish it from **social loafing**, which concerns reduced individual effort when contributions are pooled in a group.

**Apply:** the classic cyclist question points to Triplett; a general performance effect in others' presence points to social facilitation; reduced individual effort in collective work points to social loafing.

**Retrieve:** Who is named in the cyclist item? How do facilitation and loafing differ?

**Source trace:** REVISATHON Part 6, Topic 53; Unit 8, Groups and Social Influence. Use the uploaded textbooks to deepen the mechanism before treating the lesson as fully sourced.
